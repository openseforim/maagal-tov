Background for the reader

The Ḥida's second mission, 1772–1778: the history, places and everyday things behind the diary

Ḥayyim Yosef David Azulai, the Ḥida (1724–1806), kept his diary for himself. He does not explain why a rabbi from Hebron was collecting money in Venice, what a "piece" or a "sequin" was worth, why he sat in a lazaretto before he could enter Livorno, or why he dates a visit to a Paris library by the weekly Torah reading. This note gives the background a reader today is most likely to need. It follows topics, not the order of the diary.

Written 24-Nov-2026 with Claude (Anthropic). What it says about the diary is checked against the English translation of parts 1–5 (scans 7–32 of the 1885 printing); the general history comes from the works listed at the end. Corrections are welcome on GitHub.

1. The Ḥida and his two missions

Azulai was born in Jerusalem in 1724 into a family of Moroccan origin with a long line of rabbis and kabbalists; his mother's father, Joseph Bialer, was an Ashkenazi who had come to the Land of Israel with the group of Judah Ḥasid in 1700. He studied with the leading Jerusalem scholars of his youth, among them Ḥayyim ibn Attar (the Or ha-Ḥayyim), and later belonged to the circle of kabbalists around Shalom Sharabi.

He left the Land of Israel twice as an emissary of the Jews of Hebron:

  • 1753–1758: Egypt, Italy, Germany, Holland, France and England. In Amsterdam he was offered, and refused, the post of rabbi of the Sephardi community.
  • 1772/73–1778: Egypt, Tunisia, Italy, France and Holland. This is the journey of the parts of the diary in this reader. Between the missions he served as a judge in Jerusalem, spent some years as a rabbi in Cairo (1764–1769), and then settled in Hebron.

At the end of the second mission he stayed in Livorno, married again (in 1778; his first wife, Rachel, died in 1773, and part 1 opens with the news reaching him in Tunis), and spent his last 28 years there printing his books. He died in Livorno on 1 March 1806. In 1960 his remains were moved to Jerusalem.

He is remembered as a halakhist (Birkei Yosef, his glosses on the Shulḥan Arukh, which he mentions in part 3) and, above all, as the first great bibliographer of Hebrew literature: Shem ha-Gedolim lists authors and Va'ad la-Ḥakhamim lists books. Much of what went into those works was gathered on these journeys, which is why the diary stops so often in libraries.

2. Hebron and the emissary system

Why Hebron needed money. The Jewish communities of the Land of Israel under Ottoman rule (Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias) were small and poor, and they lived largely on gifts from the diaspora. They also carried heavy debts: communities borrowed from local Muslim lenders to pay taxes, bribes and extortions, and the interest grew faster than they could repay it. The Hebron community of the Ḥida's time was mainly Sephardi and devoted to study; its scholars depended on what came from abroad.

Istanbul. From the 1720s, after the Ashkenazi community of Jerusalem collapsed under its debts, a committee of the Sephardi leaders of Istanbul, the "Officials for the Land of Israel in Constantinople" (Pekidei Eretz Yisrael be-Kushta), supervised the finances of the Holy Land communities. The Ḥida mentions letters to Constantinople and disputes in which their authority is in the background.

The emissary. The usual means of raising money was the shadar (sheliaḥ de-rabbanan, "emissary of the rabbis"), a scholar sent abroad for several years with letters of appointment. The emissary was a public figure: he preached, answered legal questions, judged disputes, and was treated, in the Ḥida's words, "like a king" in the places he visited. He was also a collector, and he was paid a share of what he raised. The diary shows the three sources of money:

  • The charity box (kuppah). Many communities kept a box for the Land of Israel, and the emissary claimed what had built up in it. In Avignon the treasurer told him the box was empty and the community was owed money (part 4).
  • The community's grant, voted by its council (va'ad) or wardens (memunim, parnasim), sometimes as an "advance" against future boxes.
  • Individuals. Collecting "from individuals", house by house, needed the council's permission, and councils did not like to give it, as in Florence (part 2) and Trieste (part 4).

The system depended on trust across great distances, and it often strained it. Communities resented emissaries who stayed too long or asked too much. The Ḥida blames one predecessor for the cold welcome he met in Avignon, and he records quarrels among the rabbis of Jerusalem that damaged the name of all emissaries (part 2). Matthias Lehmann's Emissaries from the Holy Land (2014) describes this network as a form of "pan-Judaism" that tied Jews from Tunis to Amsterdam to the Holy Land.

3. The map of the second journey

The parts in this reader move through very different political worlds.

Tunis. A province of the Ottoman Empire in name, ruled in fact by its own Husainid beys. The Jewish community was divided between the old local Jews and the "Livornese" (Grana), merchants of Italian and Sephardi origin. A Jewish qaid (Arabic qā'id), a leader appointed by the bey, spoke for the Jews at court; the Ḥida dines with the qaid Solomon Nataf (part 1).

Tuscany: Livorno, Pisa, Florence. The Grand Duchy of Tuscany, ruled since 1737 by the house of Habsburg-Lorraine. Livorno was a free port, and from the 1590s its charters (the Livornine) had given Jews unusual freedom: no ghetto, the right to trade and own property, and self-government. It was the richest Sephardi community in Italy and a centre of Hebrew printing, and the Ḥida's own books of these years were printed there. Ships from plague-prone ports had to wait out a quarantine in the lazaretto before passengers could land; the Ḥida spent his in the Livorno lazaretto receiving visitors and writing (part 1).

The Papal States: Ancona, Senigallia, the Romagna. Here Jews lived in ghettos under strict papal law, and the Ḥida notes the fear caused by new decrees of the Pope (part 1); the edict of Pius VI in 1775 renewed the old restrictions. Ancona, a port on the Adriatic, had a mixed Sephardi and Italian community with its own disputes, which the Ḥida spent months settling (part 2).

Venice. The Republic of Venice was still independent, though past its height. Its Jews lived in the ghetto (from 1516) and were organized in "nations": Levantine, Ponentine (western Sephardi) and German, each with its own synagogue; the Ḥida prays "in the Levantine holy congregation" (part 3). He describes the sights a visitor was shown: the Doge's palace, the Rialto and its bank (the Banco del Giro), the Arsenal with its permanent workforce, and the Bucintoro, the state galley from which the Doge "married" the sea with a ring each year on Ascension Day (part 2).

Trieste. A Habsburg free port from 1719, growing fast and open to Jewish merchants; the Ḥida found an active community there in January 1777 (part 4).

Modena, Reggio and Mantua. Modena and Reggio belonged to the duchy of the Este family, Mantua to the Habsburgs. All three had old Italian Jewish communities with rich libraries. Mantua was the city of the great Italian kabbalists Menaḥem Azariah of Fano (d. 1620) and Moses Zacuto (d. 1697), whose graves the Ḥida visited (part 3).

France: Avignon and Paris. Jews had been expelled from the kingdom of France in 1394, and in the 1770s they had no legal community in Paris. They lived there on sufferance under police supervision: some hundreds of Sephardim from Bordeaux, Jews from Avignon and Ashkenazim from Alsace and Lorraine. Avignon and the surrounding Comtat Venaissin, on the other hand, belonged to the Pope; their Jews, "the Pope's Jews", lived in four communities (Avignon, Carpentras, Cavaillon and L'Isle-sur-la-Sorgue) and had their own rite. Many of their merchants had settled elsewhere in France, and the Ḥida praises the "people of Avignon" he met in other cities (part 4). In Paris a learned acquaintance took him to Versailles in January 1778, where Louis XVI and his brothers passed by and the king asked which country the visitor was ambassador of (part 5). The Jews of France received citizenship only in 1790–1791.

4. Dates, hours and the calendar

Weekly readings. The Ḥida usually dates an entry by the day of the week and the Torah portion of that week: "Wednesday of the week of Re'eh" is the Wednesday before the Sabbath on which Re'eh is read. Sometimes he adds the day of the month, and sometimes the year, "by the small count" without the thousands: התקל"ז is [5]537, that is 1776–77. The translation gives the civil date in brackets, worked out from the Hebrew calendar (diaspora readings). Where the entry gives no year, it is taken from the entries around it, and the brackets say "probably".

Evenings. The Hebrew day begins at nightfall, so "the night of Monday" (ליל ב') is what we call Sunday evening.

Leap years. A Hebrew leap year has two months of Adar. 5535 and 5537 were leap years, so "11 Adar" needs a note.

Hours. In Italy the hours were counted from nightfall (the "Italian hours"), not from midnight. "I could not sleep until seven hours" means seven hours after dark, around midnight in November; "two hours into the night" means two hours after dark.

Misprints. Some dates do not agree with themselves: the day of the month and the weekday or the portion point to different days. The diary was printed long after the Ḥida's death from copies, and figures are easily misread. The translation notes each case and gives the most likely date.

5. Money and gifts

The diary moves through many currencies, and their values changed. The ones that appear most:

  • Pieces (pezze, פייסאס): the Spanish silver "piece of eight", the trade dollar of the Mediterranean and the usual unit at Livorno.
  • Sequins (zecchini, סיקיני): Venetian gold ducats, the best-known gold coin of the Mediterranean.
  • Ducats and lire: Venetian money of account and coin; the Bragadin press paid "two hundred ducats a year" (part 2).
  • Louis d'or (לוידו"ר): the French gold coin, used in Avignon (part 4).

A gift often came in kind as well: a Trieste notable sent "a large vessel full of coffee and chocolate with nine sequins inside it" (part 4). Visitors brought chocolate, sweet bread and books. The Ḥida records sums to show how well a community did, not to keep accounts.

6. People and titles

  • Gevir (גביר): a rich and powerful man, usually a leader of the community.
  • Parnas, memunim, va'ad: the elected or appointed wardens and council that ran a community's affairs and money.
  • Signor (סי'): the Italian title of respect that the Ḥida uses for laymen; rabbis are "the sage" (ח"ר) or "our teacher" (מוהר"ר, כמה"ר).
  • Sandak: the man who holds the child at a circumcision, a great honor; the Ḥida is often asked, and children are named after him.
  • Maskil: in Italian communities, a scholarly title granted by the rabbis, not yet the "enlightened" man of the next century.
  • Blessings and abbreviations: names of the living carry wishes (הי"ו "may God keep him", יצ"ו "may his Rock keep him"), names of the dead carry blessings (ז"ל, זלה"ה). The translation renders them briefly or leaves them out.

7. Travel and health

The Ḥida travelled by sailing ship, by hired carriage and by the public post, by gondola in Venice, and once in a sedan chair sent by the wardens of Trieste. Travel was slow and dangerous: storms at sea, a Russian ship met off Tunis in wartime (the Russo-Turkish war of 1768–1774), a carriage overturned near Ancona and another broke on arrival at Avignon. He prays before each journey and thanks God after it; once he decides whether to go himself by drawing a biblical verse as a lot (part 2).

He suffered often from what he calls "the black sickness" (ha-shehorah), melancholy, black bile in the medicine of the time: low spirits, headaches, sleeplessness. Physicians bled him and gave him rhubarb as a purge (parts 3 and 4). After recovering he said the blessing ha-gomel.

8. Books, libraries and print

Wherever he went, the Ḥida asked to see manuscripts and early prints, and noted them: tractates of the Talmud on parchment in a library in Florence (part 2), sixteen volumes of Cordovero's Or Yakar copied in Safed in 1582, fifteenth-century Hebrew prints in Reggio (part 3), and in Paris the royal library with its "several thousand" manuscripts (part 5). These notes went into Shem ha-Gedolim. He also printed his own books while travelling, mainly in Livorno, and visited the Bragadin press in Venice, one of the oldest Hebrew presses, then run by a Jewish manager for the Venetian noble family whose name it carried (part 2).

Christian scholars sought him out as well. In Paris a member of the Académie des Sciences discussed kabbalah with him (part 4), and the Ḥida notes, with some amusement, that "the scholars of Edom" asked about him.

9. Kabbalah, segullot and the world of belief

The Ḥida was a kabbalist of Sharabi's school, and the supernatural is part of daily life in the diary. He prays at the graves of the Mantuan kabbalists, interprets his dreams, decides by lot, and gives a woman a written Shema Yisrael to swallow, a segullah (protective remedy) of Menaḥem Azariah of Fano, so that she would not leave Judaism (part 4). At the same time he is a careful legal thinker: asked in Tunis whether some local Freemasons may be killed, he answers that it is forbidden (part 1), and he argues against a ruling of Rabbi Ishmael ha-Kohen of Modena on smallpox inoculation (part 3), a medical practice then hotly debated among Italian rabbis.

10. How the text reached us, and how to read this edition

The diary was not printed in the Ḥida's lifetime. Parts of it appeared in Livorno in 1879, edited by Elijah Benamozegh, and in Przemyśl in 1885 (Sefer Ma'agal Tov). The full text was edited by Aron Freimann as Ma'agal Tov ha-Shalem for the Mekize Nirdamim society (Berlin 1921, completed Jerusalem 1934). An English translation by Benjamin Cymerman appeared in 1997; it is in copyright and is not used here.

This reader follows the Przemyśl printing, which holds part of the second journey, from Tunis on. Its scan (archive.org, University of Illinois) lacks the title page and begins in the middle of a passage. The Hebrew was read with OCR and checked line by line against the scan; spelling and abbreviations are kept as printed. The English follows the Hebrew paragraph by paragraph; dates, biblical phrases and identifications added by the translator are in gray brackets, and uncertain renderings are marked ⚠ with a note at the foot of each part.

The full diary (both journeys) follows Freimann's edition, read from the National Library of Israel copy. In that copy a corner of pp. 119–120 is torn away and a few lines on pp. 50, 51 and 80 are covered by a stain or a repair strip; these words were restored from a second copy of the same edition (HebrewBooks 21838). For the Paris weeks of 1777–78 the readings were also checked against Marx and Liber's transcription from the autograph (1913, below).
